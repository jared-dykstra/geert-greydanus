Isaac (Izak) Leo Palache, known as Leo

Jewish prisoner whom Geert helped carry on the death march (Ben's account); visited in 1959. Steven Fenves's transcript does not name him. Geert's own 1945 form says that in Niederorschel he was "met 1 Hollander, niet-jood en 4 Holl. joden" and that, apart from Akker of Leeuwarden, "Anderen allen terug".

Leo Palache is Isaac Leo Palache (Amsterdam, 20 Jan 1926 – Amsterdam, 9 Aug 1996), youngest son of Prof. Jehuda (Juda) Lion Palache, professor of Semitic languages at the University of Amsterdam, and Sophia Wilhelmina de Pinto. The Joods Monument lists him as a survivor; his parents, brother Mozes Herman and sister Judith Anna were murdered. The family address in Feb 1941 was Schubertstraat 52, Amsterdam. His father went Westerbork → Theresienstadt on 26 Feb 1944 (transport XXIV/4) and Theresienstadt → Auschwitz on 16 Oct 1944 (transport Er) (holocaust.cz); Wikipedia says the parents were murdered in Auschwitz on 18 Oct 1944 and "Leo" survived. Leo gave a USC Shoah Foundation testimony (VHA 4503, 22 Aug 1995, 1 h 54 min) whose index lists Westerbork, Auschwitz II-Birkenau, Niederorschel and Buchenwald, liberated by US forces at Buchenwald: the same subcamp and march as Geert.

The Buchenwald file for "PALACHE Izak, 20.1.1923" (Arolsen 01010503 001.376.098: no. 95755, "Pol. Holl. Jude", from Auschwitz 19 Nov 1944, "Schlosser"; one card has a handwritten note ending "Niederorschel") is him: same first name (Izak = Isaac), same day and month of birth, and the US Army roll of Dutch prisoners in Buchenwald spells the same number out as "Palache, Isaac Leo 95755" (see 11 Apr 1945). Interpretation, not a source: the birth year 1923 instead of 1926 (age 21 instead of 18) and the trade "locksmith" may be details he gave to seem fit for work. The birthplace on the cards reads "Leiden" or "Leider"; his real birthplace was Amsterdam. He is the "Palache" of the 3 May 1945 note.

Family account (Linda Walinga): Wietske said the three Dutchmen in Geert's barracks were Geert, Leo Palache and a Roman Catholic political prisoner whose name she didn't know, and that "both Pake and the other man urged Leo Palache on, dragged him along, thereby saving his life." The Catholic is almost certainly Johannes Antonius (Anton) Schepen ("Rel.: rk", Arolsen 7027532). This agrees with Ben's account of Geert helping Leo on the march. Both come from within the family, so they are not fully independent.

2020 Yad Vashem proposal (Jurjen Hoekstra with Ben Greydanus, Jenny Krabbe and Jared Dykstra, Renkum, 1 Sep 2020; sources/YadVashem_JeroensVersion.pdf): "When Leo was at the point of collapse, he was helped by Geert G., together with another Dutchman. Had he not been helped, Leo Palache would for sure have died." It quotes Leo's Shoah Foundation interview on his condition during the march: "I understand your question .... Apparently not that bad, otherwise I would have been lying by the side of the road. Yes ... injured feet, dried out, dragging ... but I completed the march." The proposal also says Leo "went to Rotterdam where he was active in the Jewish community" and that Geert "visited Leo P. in Rotterdam". The other sources place his life after the war in Amsterdam: the NIW, 16 Aug 1996, "In memoriam Leo Palache" describes his long work for the Collectieve Israel Actie and the Portuguese-Israelite community of Amsterdam, and the Joods Monument shows him blowing the shofar in Amsterdam's Portuguese Synagogue around 1960.

Ben interview; Times of Israel

Geert and Leo Palache in the interview

  • Neither is named. The interview names no Dutch political prisoner, no policeman and no Palache. The only prisoners from the Netherlands Fenves mentions are "two brothers, Hungarian Jews deported from Holland, diamond grinders by trade" who ground spectacle lenses from milk-bottle bottoms (p. 98); in 2017 he called them diamond grinders "reported from Belgium" (2017 transcript, p. 10). They are not Leo, who was born in Amsterdam.

  • Leo's own testimony. Leo Palache's Shoah Foundation interview, recorded in Dutch in Noord-Holland on 22 August 1995 (USHMM catalogue; VHA 4503, four tapes), describes Niederorschel and the march but names no fellow prisoner on it: not Geert, not Schepen and no other Dutchman. Yad Vashem reached the same reading: it "does not mention Geert Greydanus" (Yad Vashem reply). Leo remembers a shorter march, four or five days at the end of March with 200 or 250 prisoners, and describes himself on it with wounded feet, dehydrated and hungry, dragging himself along (VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:01:01–0:01:26; see the timeline, 1–10 Apr 1945). He does not say whether anyone helped him, but this is the state of a prisoner who needed help to keep up. The account that Geert and another Dutchman kept Leo going comes from the family (Ben; Wietske through Linda Walinga) and from Hoekstra, who writes that "Together they ensure that Leo reaches Buchenwald" (Hoekstra 2020, p. 26).

  • What it confirms. That Geert's column was marched for ten days, held each night inside barbed wire, and that those who fell behind were shot. That is the setting of the family account that Geert and another Dutchman, almost certainly Schepen, dragged Leo along (see the timeline, 1–10 Apr 1945).

  • Where it differs from the records. Fenves says the plant made Messerschmitt wings; the camp records and his own USHMM biography say Junkers (see conflicts). He says the camp started in spring 1944; the museum and Geert's card date its founding to September 1944 (p. 100; Arolsen 6004386). And he recalls that "the French, Norwegians, Danes, Dutch, Belgians were out of there in 48 hours" after liberation (p. 112), but Geert was still in Buchenwald on 24 April, when he signed for his property (Arolsen 6004388), and reached Eindhoven on 8–9 May.

1–10 Apr 1945 (family account)

Linda Walinga, Wietske's daughter, gives her mother's account: "Mother said that in Pake's barracks in Buchenwald there were three from the Netherlands, Pake, Leo Palache and another man also a political prisoner who was a Roman Catholic. She could not remember the man's name, and possibly never knew it. It could well be this unidentified man. In everyday life a Protestant, a Jew and a Catholic probably had little interaction, Holland was very much divided, sociologically speaking, along religious lines, but in Buchenwald they were united by language and stuck together. It was during the death march toward the end of the war that both Pake and the other man urged Leo Palache on, dragged him along, thereby saving his life."

The Catholic is almost certainly Johannes Antonius (Anton) Schepen: his Buchenwald card (Arolsen 7027532) gives "Rel.: rk" (Roman Catholic) and "Häftl. Art: Niederl.", and Geert's own 1945 form names "1 Hollander, niet-jood" with him in Niederorschel. The 3 May 1945 note lists the same three: Greydanus, Schepen, Palache. (Mother's "barracks in Buchenwald" could be the Niederorschel subcamp, run from Buchenwald, or the Dutch-speakers' barracks in the main camp where Leo Palache stayed after the liberation; see Leo's account)

1–10 Apr 1945 (Leo Palache's account)In his Shoah Foundation interview, recorded in Dutch in Noord-Holland on 22 August 1995, Leo Palache places the evacuation of Niederorschel at the end of March 1945, when the area came under Allied threat. He remembers a march of four or five days to Buchenwald, with detours around places held by troops, and a column of no more than 200 or 250 prisoners at the start, of whom he believes fewer than half arrived. Those who could not keep up were left to die by the roadside, and twice he saw a prisoner break away and be shot. Asked about his physical state, he says it cannot have been terribly bad, or he too would have ended up at the roadside; he had sore, wounded feet ("wonde voeten"), was dehydrated and hungry, dragged himself along ("je voortslepend") and completed the march. He names no companion on the march. He also recalls about a month in the Buchenwald main camp before liberation, including a three-hour naked punishment roll call in snow in early March. 11 Apr – c. 7 May 1945 (Leo Palache's account)Leo dates his liberation to 10 April. Armed prisoners disarmed the SS men in the camp, and the Americans came in a day later. In Buchenwald he was in a barracks for Dutch speakers, about 60 or 70 men, where he got to know non-Jewish Dutchmen; this fits Wietske's account that the Dutch prisoners shared a barracks. Two days after liberation the whole Dutch barracks paraded, an older prisoner spoke, and a US Army band played the Wilhelmus. On 30 April, Princess Juliana's birthday, the Americans brought the barracks two crates of whisky. Around 7 May, by his recollection, US Army trucks took the Dutch to Eindhoven, where they were received in the Philips Veemgebouw, the centre Geert reached on 8–9 May (below)VHA 4503, tape 2, 0:31:22; VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:09:33–0:11:35, VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:05:48–0:06:00, VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:16:06–0:17:37, VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:17:57–0:18:25, VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:19:31
