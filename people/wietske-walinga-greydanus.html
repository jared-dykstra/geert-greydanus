Wietske Walinga-Greydanus (1924–2008) and her hunger-winter journey

Geert Greydanus's daughter Wietske: hostage in Kamp Vught in 1943, and her diary of a bicycle journey from Rotterdam to Friesland for food in the hunger winter of 1945.

Last updated 22 November 2026, 04:07 UTC

The full timeline of Geert Greydanus (1899–1965)
PersonConnection to GeertSource
Wietske Walinga-Greydanus (1924–2008)Geert's daughter and eldest child, born in Rotterdam on 4 Mar 1924 ("Grietje Wietske" on her Vught card). Held as a hostage in Kamp Vught with her mother and brothers, 2–18 Dec 1943 (no. 0667). In the hunger winter she cycled to Friesland for food, 21 Mar – 22 May 1945, and wrote the journey into the family journal (pp. 30–37), which she kept. She emigrated with the family in 1947, went by Wilma in Canada (Jared Dykstra, family account, Nov 2026), married Dirk Walinga on 27 Nov 1948, and wrote "The Greydanus History". Died 28 Jul 2008 (Hoekstra 2021)Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", pp. 1–4; Hoekstra 2021; 21–24 Mar 1945

1 Dec 1943Johanna and all five children are taken hostage (Hoekstra says four), questioned at the police station and SS headquarters, then taken to Kamp Vught. A letter from Vught of 15 December gives their numbers: Johanna 0666 and Wietske 0667 in the women's camp, Jacob 8426 and Jan 8428 in the men's camp, "terwijl de beide jongste kinderen geen no. hebben" (while the two youngest have no number). This was Rauter's policy against police desertion: the relatives of officers who went into hiding with their pistol and ammunition were to be arrested and interned in a camp. If the officer reported within four weeks, his family was freed and he was put to work in a concentration camp; if the Order Police caught him, he faced a German military court for illegally holding a firearm (Van Riet 2008, p. 546). About 150 relatives of police officers in hiding were held in Vught as hostages (Van Riet 2008, p. 547). Van Velzen's family was taken the same way at about the same time: his wife, E.M. Jonkergouw, and their three children (8, 5 and 4) were arrested at home on or about 2 December, she was questioned by the SD man Richter and told him she did not know where her husband was, though she did, and the next day she and the two eldest children were taken to Kamp Vught. They stayed about three weeks, until Rauter pardoned the families of policemen in hiding (Van Riet 2008, p. 547)Hoekstra 2013, p. 6; Geert journal p. 3a
6–15 Dec 1943Letters to and from Vught. On 6 Dec Wietske (prisoner 0667, Block 24) writes home asking for food and for sweaters for Jaap and Jan, and gives the rules: one letter every two weeks, one parcel per person per week; "Moeder, Gerard en Corrie hebben nr. 0666". On 7 Dec a helper reports seeing Cornelis and bringing a St Nicholas gift. On 12 Dec Jan writes from the men's camp: "We zijn hier nu al 10 dagen. De eerste week hoefden we niet te werken maar nu wel". 18 Dec 1943Rauter releases the police officers' families "in view of the coming Christmas season". The Vught index cards date it exactly. Johanna's reads "Greydanus geb. Pebesma, Jacobje … Gijz. 0666 … Garijp … met 2 kinderen … Binnengekomen: 2 Dec. 1943, Vrijgelaten 18 Dec. 1943" (Arolsen 360367, file 360366). Wietske's card, as "Greydanus, Grietje Wietske … Gijz. 0667 … Rotterdam, 4 Maart 1924", gives the same "Binnengekomen: 2 Dec 1943" and "vrijgelaten 18 Dec. 1943" (Arolsen 360364). "Gijz." means gijzelaar, hostage. Hoekstra: Johanna takes the youngest children to Geert's family in Anjum, and Jacob and Jan go to Lammert and Romkje Baron in Suawoude. Wietske remembers it differently: after a family reunion in Friesland in early January 1944, Gerard and Cor stayed with Tjebbe and Klaske Tibma-Greydanus in Ezumazyl, near Anjum, until after the liberation, "So only 4 of us went back to Rotterdam"; Jaap and Jan went to Friesland only in the hunger winter, after Ben's birth in October 1944 (see winter 1944–45; Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", p. 2)Rauter note no. 406/1943 + translation; Hoekstra 2013, p. 6
Early Jan 1944Geert travels to relatives in Friesland, and Johanna and the children, freed from Vught, join him there for a family reunion. Wietske: "In the beginning of January, 1944, he travelled to our relatives in Friesland and we did as well for a family reunion." Gerard and Cor stay on in Friesland; Geert goes back to the Satter family. Ben is born nine months later, on 6 Oct 1944Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", p. 2
20 Apr 1944The farm is surrounded. Geert, Hendrik Satter and Anne Schaap are arrested (Hoekstra says they were listening to the BBC). Geert had already gone to Friesland for his new "job" with the Trouw group, but "had to go back to Varsseveld once more to get his belongings" (Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", p. 2). J. Satter's 1952 letter agrees: about a week after Geert left for Friesland he came to Varsseveld for a weekend, with Hendrik Satter and Schaap, and was arrested (J. Satter letter 1952). Ben says his father had a premonition and threw his revolver and false IDs into the outhouse. The LO-LKP's Varsseveld report names the three: "On April 20, 1944, he [Markus] struck. On that date, 3 persons were taken away here, namely Satter, Greydanus, and Schaap" (English rendering on archieven.nl) (NIOD 251a inv. 20, scan 138)Verzetsrapport; Hoekstra 2013, p. 8; Ben interview; Willy Markus (Wikipedia NL)
Winter 1944–45The hunger winter in Rotterdam. Wietske: "Food rations were very scarce and often not even available. We had no coal, no gas and no hydro." By her account, Jaap and Jan now put two bicycles together and set off for Friesland; Hoekstra places their move to Friesland just after Vught (see 18 Dec 1943). The bikes did not hold up, so they walked most of the way to their great-uncle and aunt, Jan and Jetske Greydanus, in Oudeschoot ("Oudeschool" in her history), then went on to Genum, where they stayed with the Damsma and Buruma families until the liberation. When Johanna developed oedema from lack of food, the family decided that Wietske should join three other girls cycling north to look for food: "That was a very wrong decision!" Joel's essay says the family at home got through on the extra ration cards of the brothers who had leftWietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", pp. 2–3; Joel essay, p. 5
21–24 Mar 1945Wietske, aged 21, sets out by bicycle with Truus Maaskant for Friesland, and with Toos and Annerie Numan, who are bound for Ruinerwold. Her diary of the trip, written into the family journal, begins: "Met ons vieren gingen we op stap om wat eten op te halen" (the four of us set out to fetch some food). At noon on the first day, just short of Utrecht with their second flat tyre, they send a pencilled note home with a Rotterdam policeman: "Lieve Moeder & Bennie, We zitten hier even voor Utrecht met een lekke band." They reach Harderwijk that night, but the skipper who might have taken them north has just left. The next day Wietske and Truus visit Ane Huyser at Sonnevanck, and all four ride on to Heerde. Finding no ferry at Wapenveld, they cross the IJssel "in een lekke boot zonder roeispanen" (in a leaky boat without oars), drifting two river widths downstream to land near Windesheim, and sleep in the hay at farmer Kamphuis's. On the fourth day Toos and Annerie turn off at Meppel, and Wietske and Truus go on by Steenwijk and Wolvega, partly on foot and partly by horse and cart, to reach Oudeschoot at 9 in the eveningWietske's diary, journal p. 30, 30b, 32, 33; English translation (Hoekstra 2021); Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", p. 3
26 Mar – Apr 1945With two tyres from Oom Jan, Wietske and Truus cycle on to Cubaard, going round the farms near Irnsum for about a pound of butter each, and the next day to Anjum, where her brothers Gerard and Cor, staying nearby at Ezumazyl, ask first: "Hoe is het met Bensje?" (how is little Ben?). Going home is too risky, so they spend Easter in Anjum. Her diary dates the liberation of Friesland to 13 April, when the N.B.S. occupied the Schans. Truus stays at the Kooiplaats in Anjum; Wietske moves between Anjum and Genum. On 23 April the evacuation bureau at Metslawier registers "Greijdanus, Wietske geb. 4.3.'24 te R'dam" in the municipality of Oostdongeradeel as a "hongervluchteling" (hunger refugee)Wietske's diary, journal p. 33, 34; hunger-refugee certificate, journal p. 35b; English translation (Hoekstra 2021); Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", p. 2
1–10 Apr 1945 (family account)

Linda Walinga, Wietske's daughter, gives her mother's account: "Mother said that in Pake's barracks in Buchenwald there were three from the Netherlands, Pake, Leo Palache and another man also a political prisoner who was a Roman Catholic. She could not remember the man's name, and possibly never knew it. It could well be this unidentified man. In everyday life a Protestant, a Jew and a Catholic probably had little interaction, Holland was very much divided, sociologically speaking, along religious lines, but in Buchenwald they were united by language and stuck together. It was during the death march toward the end of the war that both Pake and the other man urged Leo Palache on, dragged him along, thereby saving his life."

The Catholic is almost certainly Johannes Antonius (Anton) Schepen: his Buchenwald card (Arolsen 7027532) gives "Rel.: rk" (Roman Catholic) and "Häftl. Art: Niederl.", and Geert's own 1945 form names "1 Hollander, niet-jood" with him in Niederorschel. The 3 May 1945 note lists the same three: Greydanus, Schepen, Palache. (Mother's "barracks in Buchenwald" could be the Niederorschel subcamp, run from Buchenwald, or the Dutch-speakers' barracks in the main camp where Leo Palache stayed after the liberation; see Leo's account)

Linda Walinga, statement to Jared, Nov 2026 (from Wietske Walinga-Greydanus); Arolsen 7027532; Greydanus 2b
11 Apr – c. 7 May 1945 (Leo Palache's account)Leo dates his liberation to 10 April. Armed prisoners disarmed the SS men in the camp, and the Americans came in a day later. In Buchenwald he was in a barracks for Dutch speakers, about 60 or 70 men, where he got to know non-Jewish Dutchmen; this fits Wietske's account that the Dutch prisoners shared a barracks. Two days after liberation the whole Dutch barracks paraded, an older prisoner spoke, and a US Army band played the Wilhelmus. On 30 April, Princess Juliana's birthday, the Americans brought the barracks two crates of whisky. Around 7 May, by his recollection, US Army trucks took the Dutch to Eindhoven, where they were received in the Philips Veemgebouw, the centre Geert reached on 8–9 May (below)VHA 4503, tape 2, 0:31:22; VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:09:33–0:11:35, VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:05:48–0:06:00, VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:16:06–0:17:37, VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:17:57–0:18:25, VHA 4503, tape 3, 0:19:31
14 Apr 1945A handwritten copy of the prison poem "Ik heb zooveel geleerd…", about faith in solitary confinement ("in „Einzelhaft" van alles schier beroofd"), is signed "Varsseveld 14 April 1945", two weeks after Varsseveld, near the Satter farm where Geert had hidden, was liberated. It is kept with Wietske Walinga's papers; the copyist is not named. The note under it credits the poem to Adolf Snijders, born 14 January 1920, written in prison in Utrecht and "gefusilleerd 26 Juli 1943". Snijders, of the resistance intelligence group Inlichtingen Dienst, was executed at the Leusderheide on 20 July 1943 according to the Oorlogsgravenstichting. The poem is printed in the Geuzenliedboek 1940–1945 as "Ter dood veroordeeld"Handwritten copy + transcription and translation; Oorlogsgravenstichting: Adolf Snijders; Geuzenliedboek no. 38
May 1945With the liberation comes a ban on travel, and Wietske can get no permit; her rear tyre has given out on the way to Genum, and Truus has gone home through Drenthe. Wietske gets a lift with two American soldiers bound for Haarlem, across the Afsluitdijk, which civilians are not allowed to cross. A round-up of Germans ("moffenrazzia") in the Wieringermeer stops her; she has to stay with the N.B.S. at Hippolytushoef and is then lodged with Melle Montsma. On Whit Monday, 21 May, she sets off in pouring rain with about 40 kg on the back of her bicycle, through Purmerend to Amsterdam, and is home the next day (see 22 May 1945)Wietske's diary, journal p. 35, 36; English translation (Hoekstra 2021); Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", p. 3
After liberationGeert collects his sons from Friesland unannounced; Jerry recalls "he just showed up". For how Wietske learned her father was alive, see 22 May 1945Ben interview (Jerry); Context doc; 30ff translation
22 May 1945Wietske gets home, ill with jaundice. Her diary: "s'Avonds om 8 uur thuis. Vader was toen al meer als een week thuis." The two accounts differ on how she learned her father was alive: her diary says she read it in an old issue of Trouw in Purmerend; her later "Greydanus History" says a family showed her a newspaper list of Buchenwald survivors with her father's nameWietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History"; Wietske's diary, journal p. 36, p. 37

By May 1954Geert lives on a farm near Clinton, Ont. His Canadian citizenship certificate of 28 May 1954 gives his address as "CLINTON, ONTARIO, CANADA" and his occupation as "FARMER" (Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History", pp. 7–8). Hoekstra and Wietske date the purchase to 1955; Wietske: "In 1955, father and mother bought a farm near Clinton for $3500" (p. 4). Ben recalls it as "something like the 13th move in Canada"Ben interview; Hoekstra 2013, p. 19
1959Geert uses the lump sum for a three-month trip to the Netherlands with Johanna; Wietske calls it "a three months vacation by boat to Holland" (Wietske Walinga, "The Greydanus History"). A Holland-America Line passage list for the Ryndam sailing of 5 Oct 1959 has "Greydanus G.", two fares, New York to Southampton, booked through Toronto (Stadsarchief Rotterdam 318-04 inv. 1164, scan 75). He visits the Satter family and Leo Palache, then 33. Hendrik Satter himself had died on 16 Oct 1949 (Oorlogsgravenstichting); Hoekstra quotes Wil Satter remembering the visit and Geert's red-lined Canadian jacket. Ben, from his mother: on a train during the trip Geert saw a man he felt had wronged him in the war, and it bothered him so much that they got off the train; Ben thought the name was something like "Kohlabrander", but "Can't put any money on that"Hoekstra 2013, p. 19; Ben interview
