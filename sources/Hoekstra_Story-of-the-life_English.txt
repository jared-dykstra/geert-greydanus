Story of the life of Geert Greydanus
The eventful life of a special person


Introduction to History.

In 2005 I started writing down the history of my grandparents: Jurjen Baron and Jeltje Postma. They lived from around 1880 to 1950.
In addition to the First World War, they also experienced the Second World War. In the latter war they gave shelter to Leni de Beer, a Jewish woman from Rotterdam.
What particularly intrigued me was the coexistence of Leni (the Jewess) de Beer and Jurjen en Jeltje. How did they manage that, living together?
It was also important for me to know more about Leni de Beer, what kind of person she actually was. After a long search, I tracked down the main details of her life.
The presentation of the Yad Vashem medal to Jurjen Baron and Jeltje Postma, posthumously of course, to show appreciation for their help to this Jewish woman, was the crowning glory of my work. This ceremony took place in the Liberal Jewish Synagogue in Amsterdam on December 15, 2011.

But one question remained on my mind: how did Leni de Beer get from Rotterdam to the small town of Suawoude?

The first step to find out was to obtain information from the children of Reverend Pel, the Reformed pastor in Suawoude. It was common knowledge that he had meant a lot to people in hiding in Suawoude during the war. Contact with his daughter in The Hague did not result in any meaningful information. Ds. Pel had not kept any records and did tell his children anything about the war.


You have to be lucky with genealogy.

I was in regular contact with Klaas Baron, the youngest son of Lammert Baron, the youngest brother of Jurjen Baron.
Klaas, born in 1930, had written down his childhood memories (until 1945) before he went to the vocational school in Leeuwarden.
In his story he tells a lot about the war, which he, as a teenager, had very consciously experienced.
It so happened that Klaas wrote something about cousin Geert. And it slowly dawned on me that he meant Geert Greydanus.
During the war Klaas Baron had several times been in contact with Geert, but also with cousin Wiebe - son of Gerrit Baron, again a brother of Jurjen Baron.
Both Wiebe and Gerrit were active in the resistance.
And there it was, the link between Leni de Beer, Geert Greydanus and Wiebe.
After all, both Leni and Geert lived in Rotterdam.


Who was Geert Greydanus and what is the connection with the Baron family?

Geert was married to Johanna (Jacobje) Pebesma. Johanna was the third child of Jan Pebesma and Wietske Baron. This Wietske was the oldest sister of Jurjen Baron (Pake)
Wietske and Jan had emigrated to Germany in 1906 to work in the mines at Recklinghausen. In 1916 they returned to the Netherlands, where Jan got a job in the coalmines in Limburg.
Wietske-muoi (= aunt Wietske) had always kept contact with the family. And to this day there are still contacts with her relatives. It was therefore logical that Johanna also regularly visited Suawoude. During one of these visits she may have come into contact with Geert Greydanus who lived in Friesland until after his marriage.
For me it meant rooting in Geert's life, especially his life during the war years. Here's the story:


The childhood of Geert Greydanus

Geert Greydanus was born in Kubaard on December 1, 1899. He came from a family of 9 children, of which he was the second. His parents were: Jacob Greydanus and Saakje Hibma.
Kubaard was the perfect place to grow up as a child. Playing in the street, because there was not much traffic.


Old postcard from Cubaard


After primary school Geert went to work for a farmer until he had to do military service.
He ended up with the military police. This was a great stepping-stone for Geert. After military service he applied for a job with the police in Rotterdam, and he was indeed hired.

In his bachelor time, Geert, like all young men of his age, goes out.
At one point he meets a Frisian girl, Johanna (Jacobje) Pebesma who tells him that she lived with her parents in Limburg.
She was the third daughter of a family of 9 children. Her parents were Jan Pebesma and Wietske Baron. Jan Pebesma was a farm-worker from Garijp.
Times were bad around 1906 and Jan and Wietske decided to move to Germany.
Johanna was one of the 5 children the couple had at that time.
It was quite an undertaking at the time, but work in the mines meant a good income, although the work was heavy and bad for your health.
Jan Pebesma, for example, settled with his family in Recklinghausen, not far from the Dutch border.
Wietske missed her family and kept in close contact with her brothers and sisters, and of course with her parents. Her father died in 1914 and her mother in 1928.
Jan and Wietske did not want to stay permanently in Recklinghausen and they decided to move back to the Netherlands. Jan applied for work in the Dutch coal-mines. He ended up in Treebeek and later in Brunssum.
During the period that the family lived in Recklinghausen, three more children were born. Their youngest child was born in Limburg.

As mentioned earlier, there was always a strong bond with the family in Friesland. The children went back and forth “út fan hûs” (staying with family).
It is possible and indeed likely that Johanna Pebesma from Limburg met Geert Greydanus during one of her visits to Friesland. Slowly a relation build as they started writing each other. And a few times a year Johanna came to Friesland and Geert came to Limburg.


Departure to Rotterdam

Geert and Johanna married on May 9, 1923 in Heerlen. Geert had in the meantime successfully applied with the police in Rotterdam. Before the war, Geert had already been promoted twice: first to sergeant, then to major. In this position, he worked as a police-detective.
Geert and Johanna moved several times with their family, with 5 children, in Rotterdam.
Their first child was named Wietske, born March 4, 1924.
Then Jacob, born January 17, 1927; Jan 4 June 1928; Gerard Bouke on February 15, 1932 and Cornelis Wiebe on November 6, 1936.
(Bernard was born during the war, but about that later.)
Finally they got a house at West Varkenoordseweg 359 B. A modern, spacious house on the outskirts of the city, with spectacular unobstructed views as far as IJsselmonde.


Then war broke out in 1940. Anti-Jewish measures

Geert and Johanna are spectators of the fierce battle for the bridges in Rotterdam and, after a few days, also of the terrible bombardment of the center. Eight hundred deaths and many more seriously injured are the result.
80,000 people were made homeless. 24,000 Houses, 32 churches and 2 synagogues were destroyed. The Netherlands was thus forced into surrender.

Leni de Beer must also have experienced this heavy bombardment. She lived close to the center in the Breitnerstraat, a few hundred meters to the west of the bombarded parts.

Difficult times begin. How will they go on? Geert and Johanna try to pick up the thread again.

The possibilities for Jewish people are limited. Leni is fired from the Erasmiaans Gymnasium, but luckily is re-hired at the Jewish Lyceum.

Slowly the resistance-movement is taking shape. The Dutch people cannot stomach the fact that so much injustice is being done. Not long after the start of the war, the first resistance newspaper (Vrij Nederland) appears.


The arrest of Jews and people in hiding - accept or protest?

More and more Jewish people are arrested, and initially transported to Westerbork and then further on. Nobody knows what that "further on" means. Every now and then mail comes from distant Poland, where they are said to have been taken. But there isn't much information. Many Jews try to escape, in all thinkable ways, but the possibilities to do so are becoming increasingly limited.

And Geert? Geert doesn't know what to do. Should he accept what's going on? Or protest? Or resign?

At this stage of the war, it is already clear that people who obstruct or resist the Germans are treated harshly and often executed immediately. Can he do this to his family?
And many of his colleagues even actively cooperate in the arrest of Jewish people and people in hiding.
At first Geert also participates, but this goes strongly against his nature. He decides to go into hiding himself and maybe join the organized resistance against the Germans.
He discusses this with Johanna and she understands that he is right, but obviously fears the possible consequences.
Early 1943 the leadership of the policeforce announces that participating in actions on behalf of the Germans is mandatory, with the threat of dismissal for those who do not participate.


Hiding in the Achterhoek - the Satter farm

Geert decides to go into hiding immediately.
His contacts with the newly founded resistance newspaper Trouw bring him into contact with Jo Satter. Jo Satter was involved early on in the distribution of this illegal newspaper Trouw. Jo also involved father Hendrik in the resistance. His father often made his farm in Sinderen available as an intermediate shelter for Jews and other people in hiding.
Geert was warmly welcomed by the Satter family, and he became a close family friend. It was in his nature to help as much as possible on the farm, where many activities took place.
The Trouw newspaper was founded in Aerdenhout in 1943. To spread the risk, meetings also took place in the east of the country, also at Hendrik Satter's farm. Hendrik Satter's daughter, Willemien, was not allowed to attend these meetings, nor was Geert Greydanus. The fewer people were informed, the safer it was, especially for the people in hiding on the farm. The resistance newspaper Trouw was gradually distributed throughout the whole of the Netherlands.

On the photo Geert left, next to Hendrik Satter. On the right two people in hiding and a father of a person in hiding. In the middle two children of Hendrik, Jas and Dinie and before Wil and Henk.
(collection of Willemien Satter)

It will certainly have been a change for Geert.

Every now and then Geert and Johanna can meet and be together for a while. Sometimes on the farm, sometimes elsewhere.
After some time Johanna becomes pregnant. Hendrik Satter was angry when Geert told him this. "How did you get that stupid idea into your head? If the Germans find out that Johanna is pregnant, they will understand or at least presume that Johanna knows where you are!" Bernard is born on October 6, 1944.

Geert helped on the farm, he was not the man to sit still. That did not fit with his Christian upbringing. He also did other odd jobs for Hendrik Satter. It is not entirely clear which these off jobs were, because little was said or written. The less you knew, the less danger. Geert was not actively involved in the actual resistance work from the farm. This was too risky as he was a wanted person.
Geert had a lot of humor. Willemien Satter remembered that Geert chewed tobacco and sometimes deliberately placed a piece of chewing-tobacco somewhere. Hendrik Satter did not appreciate this very much, but could see the humor in it.


Johanna and the children hostage

Then disaster strikes. Johanna is taken hostage with their 4 children on December 1, 1943 by the Germans. They were transported to the police station for interrogation. The police wanted to know where Geert was hiding.
They spend the first night in a cell at the police station. The next day they are taken to the SS headquarters where they were again interrogated. A day later they were taken to camp Vught. By former colleagues of Geert! Again they were interrogated to find out the hiding-place of Geert.
Apparently the Germans were really interested in getting hold of Geert. A senior police-detective possibly joining the resistance. That was of course not acceptable to the occupier. The Germans try everything to get Johanna and the kids talking. They are known to have used very harsh methods to get information from detainees.

Geert panics when he finds out that his family has been arrested. He decides to turn himself in. But Hendrik Satter and Anne Schaap (future son-in-law of Hendrik) in the end convince him not to do this. They predict that Germans will eventually let Johanna and the children go. But that his life is in danger when he turns himself in.
It was clear to him that he had to leave the farm, because if Johanna starts talking to the Germans, Hendrik Satter's group would also be in danger.
Geert and Willemien Satter and her fiancé Anne Schaap take the train and bus to Beetgum. It is a precaution that Willemien Satter and her fiancé travel with Geert, to prevent Geert, who is very upset, from reporting to the Germans after all. In Beetgum he finds shelter in the courier-business of Anne Schaap's parents. In the quiet Frisian village of Beetgum Geert start to relax again. And after a few weeks, when all is clear, Geert returns to Sinderen.

In the meantime, what Anne Schaap and Hendrik Satter already predicted happened. Just before Christmas Johanna and her 4 children are released by the highest authority Rauter. She decides to go to Friesland for some rest and maybe to see Geert again. She is staying with the two youngest children with family members of Geert, who now live in Anjum. The two oldest boys, aged fifteen and sixteen, find shelter with Lammert and Romkje in Suawoude.


A little anecdote.

Jan and Jaap, Geert and Johanna's oldest boys, have been shaved bald in Vught. They were housed in the men's camp while Johanna was in the women's camp with the two youngest children.
On Sundays Lammert and Romkje went to church with the children.
Of course, wearing a cap in the church was irreverent, and a shaven head in the church was also disrespectful. Lammert talked about it with reverend Pel, who had no objection when the boys came to church wearing a cap. And so it happened.


Activities at Hendrik Satter's farm

What else happened on the farm?
Jo Satter, son of Hendrik Satter, is mainly occupied as a member of the national Trouw resistance group, while Anne Schaap takes care of the Jewish families.
Sometimes the farm is used as an intermediate station for downed pilots, on their way back, with some luck, to England.

Since Geert is a religious man and he cannot go to church on Sundays, he occasionally conducted a secret meditation service in the evening, when people in hiding came together on the farm.

Geert also visits Suawoude now and than. Klaas Baron has seen him there several times. It was not difficult for Geert to find shelter because the family of Johanna lived in Suawoude. In order not to stand out too much, he often disguises himself. Klaas Baron remembers that he once came cycling into Suawoude dressed as an old farmer.

The family in Suawoude visited by Geert included Jurjen Baron with his wife Jeltje. They lived in a small farm. This place turns out to be an ideal hiding place. Thus it happened that Leni de Beer, a Jewish teacher from Rotterdam, was housed there for a year and a half and survived the war.

Lammert Baron, a younger brother of Jurjen, also lived in Suawoude with his wife Romkje. They gave another Jewess long-term shelter.



The small house where Jurjen and Jeltje lived. The Jewish teacher Leni de Beer lived here for a year and a half. Geert will undoubtedly have visited several times.
(Jurjen Hoekstra Collection)


Both brothers (Jurjen and Lammert) were Johanna's uncles.
There were many more people in hiding in that small, inconspicuous village of Suawoude. Reverend Pel, the Reformed minister there, did a lot of good work.

After staying with the Satter family for about a year, the inevitable happens and disaster strikes!

April 20, 1944 raid on Satter's farm

The arrested go via Camp Vught to concentration-camp Siegburg and Buchenwald.

Geert visited Rotterdam that day and had just returned from the station in Varsseveld. He was dressed in black, complete with a top hat.
The Germans raid the Satter farm with 15 to 20 soldiers, clearly focussed on the Satter farm. Betrayal was thus the most likely cause. Escape is not possible. The three, Anne Schaap, Geert Greydanus and Hendrik Sattermen, were listening together to the English radio. Surprisingly Hendrik Satter manages to hide the radio.
They had previously agreed that in an emergency, Anne would act like a fool. This works and he is soon released. Later the Germans find out that they have been tricked, but by then Anne is already in hiding.
Hendrik (who is a widower) asks the Germans if he can say goodbye to his children. He feared that with his resistance history he would not return. The Germans refuse and kick him off the yard into a truck.
Hendrik Satter is deported to Vught and then to Siegburg, a concentration camp in Germany. He survived the war but comes back a human wreck who contracted typhus in the camp.

Geert is first deported to Arnhem and then to Vught. He is violently tortured in the so called "bunker". He was tortured there by: v.d Berg, Enkelstrodt, Wemelink and Popey, the last was nicknamed "the bunker executioner".
Geert manages to hold his own, and more importantly, doesn't betray anyone.
He is convicted as a political prisoner and send to Buchenwald concentration camp. Geert was lucky that he was sentenced as a political prisoner, because as a resistance fighter he would have been executed.


Buchenwald

Geert is taken by train to Buchenwald.

In the train he sees an opportunity to write a note in pencil and throw it out the window. There was obviously only a small chance of it ever reaching the addressed, but the note was duly delivered in Rotterdam and is still kept as a souvenir.
(Collection Jenny Krabbe)

Buchenwald is a labor camp wher the Germans have two factories. One for making parts for small arms and another for making parts for the V2, the Germans' infamous missile.


(Photo from Buchenwald camp brochure)

Geert ended up in the so-called small camp on 15 August 1944. The booking will not have been gentle, but in any case was done carefully. See attached form.
Striking on one of the forms is the details of the race to which Geert belongs. This was of course intended for Jews and Gypsies. But Geert proudly wrote down that he was of the Frisian race.

(Pedigree card from Buchenwald archive)

On the card it was also noted that Geert is missing some teeth and that he is hard of hearing. Willemien Satter cannot remember anything about Geert being deaf. It may have been caused by the tortures he endured in Vught.
And he had lost a lot of weight, he weighed only 65 kg.

All prisoners went to the small camp first. This was to avoid contagious diseases. When Geert Greydanus arrived in Buchenwald, there were 13,000 prisoners in this small camp. The barracks were old horse stables. There were also tents to sleep in. You can see the overpopulation in some photos from this time. Geert found a place in barrack 58. The barrack itself is no longer present, the location of the barrack is indicated with a post number 58.
In this small camp with its thousands of inhabitants, dying was the order of the day. By definition the Germans gave too little food and no medical care. If you were ill there was little chance of a cure or survival.
In the large camp the men received 800 kcal / day and in the small camp 500 kcal. When you consider that in normal circumstances a man needs at least 2500 kcal, it is clear that lack of nutrition lead to mass starvation.
Which was the intention of the Nazis.


(Left list from Buchenwald archive)


Geert was lucky. A sub-camp was to be set up near Buchenwald. Geert belonged to a group of 100 men who were taken to Niederorschel, about 90 km from Buchenwald. There were about 60 of these sub-camps around Buchenwald. The group was made up of 8 nationalities, most notably Russians. There is also another Dutchman, Jan Schepen.

On the previous page the transport list, on which the name of Geert Greydanus can be seen. He has number 5892. (Right column)
So Geert, within 3 weeks after arriving in Buchenwald, is transported to Niederorschel. This transfer probably ensured his survival.


Niederorschel

Quit a lot is known about the camp in Niederorschel. A complete book has been published of what happened. Geert belonged to the group that had to help build the camp. He was therefore involved in the work from the outset.
It was a mechanical weaving mill, which, together with the surrounding houses, must be made suitable for the production of aircraft parts for Focke Wulf fighter aircraft, the showpiece of the German Air Force.
The area is cordoned off with electrified barbed wire with a voltage of 220-280 volts. The prisoners are being watched by around 40 guards, some of which treat the prisoners very harsly.
The speed of production of the aircraft parts never reaches that of a normal factory, despite the prisoners having to work 12 hours a day, 6 days a week.
Working in the factory is no fun. The drilling of aluminum parts makes a hell of a noise. In addition, aluminum dust in the air was very unhealthy. And there was no heating during winter.
Malnutrition and as a result a bad overal condition caused many diseases, the main reason for the poor production.
Although the diet was better than in the other concentration camps, it was nowhere enough. But luck had it that the kitchen also cooked for the guards, which improved the quality for the prisoners.
In addition, the factory manager regularly had potato glue made, officially for production, but in reality for people to eat.
Jan Schepen, the fellow Dutchman, who came to Niederorschel with Geert, contracted pneumonia and was admitted to the infirmary. Although there was a doctor, medicines were scarce and Jan was left untreated. But he get better anyhow.
Jan Schepen also witnesses that, despite serious illness, Jews in the infirmary were forced out of bed in the morning to appear on roll call and go to work. The SS men were often merciless and prisoners die regularly.

The prisoners were easily distinguishable from one another. The political prisoners wore a different patch than the Jews. So it was esy for the SS-men to pick out the Jews.
Fortunately there were also good Germans, such as some inhabitants of the village who brought food to the prisoners. The technical supervisor, Andreas Schröter, was also very understanding of the prisoners and helped them wherever possible.
But doom and gloom prevailed. A Dutch electrician (maybe Jan Schepen) came into conflict with the SS-men, who thought they knew more about electricity than he did. He was beaten so badly that he spent months in the infirmary. Thanks to the extra nutrition he received from the villagers, he survived.

A special guard is Otto Hermann, a German communist. He has to do the dirty chores for the SS-men. But in fact he does everything to support the prisoners. He is a special man, a born leader. Thanks to him many people survived their stay in this concentration camp. For instance, he convinces the camp leadership that the death of prisoners makes life difficult for him. After all he has to train new people all the time, which in turn leads to bad work.

The first group, to which Geert belonged, finished the preparations in a month time. After that three transports with workers, prisoners, political refugees and prisoners of war arrive. In total, more than 700 people come to Niederorschel, among them 5 Dutch people.

Then it is March 1945. The Americans are getting closer and closer.
As early as January, the SS-men are preparing for the evacuation of the prisoners. In principle, the people who cannot handle the long march to Buchenwald must be killed before the journey begins.

On April 1, 1945, Easter Sunday, at 9 o'clock in the evening, all prisoners have attend. They must also carry with them everything they own. Their blankets, clothing and anything else, although this will not have been much.
The prisoners are terrified because they are familiar with the fact that many other "Todesmarschen" have ended in a bloodbath. Only Otto Hermann knows where the journey is going as he was in contact with people in the camp Buchenwald.
The final destination is indeed Buchenwald. A journey of about 90 km that must be walked on foot. And that although all are in bad shape.

The group walks in rows one after the other, with guards and dogs on either side.
The march starts in the night from Sunday to Monday. First they were chased out of the sleeping-quarters by a new group of SS-men, people they didn't know yet.

(Accompanying print from: Aus dem Umkreis der Kamine)

They are warned that anybody who leaves the line will be shot on the spot.
The prisoners realized only too well that the SS were serious. The commander had told the prisoners several times that none of them would survive the war. And so they are all terrified. They fear that the SS will take them to a place to be executed, which happened regularly.
They all receive a piece of bread as food. And security hands out everything that is still in the camp. The camp doctor ensured that the sick can go on a farm cart behind a tractor. And the last few medicines are taken on the march.
Pretty soon after the start, the bad footwear of the prisoners starts to have consequences. The get open wounds on their feet. These heal poorly and cannot be cared for.

Here is a map of the death march that the prisoners from Niederorschel walked.
The death march to Buchenwald is not following the shortest route. The prisoners must be kept away from the advancing Americans.

Sometimes they sleep in the open air, sometimes in a commandeered shed. More and more they must pause on their way as the weak prisoners cannot continue.
The Germans originally intended to rest during the day and to move during the night. But as the road was hard to find at night,

(Route sketch from Aus dem Umkreis der Kamine)

The Germans decide to take a risk and continue during the day.
A group of Hungarians decide to flee. A few regret it and return and they are left unharmed by the Germans. The others are captured and shot.

Marching during the day had the risk that low-flying American planes would discover and attack them. But this risk was accepted.
On the evening of April 4, 1945, there is a huge thunderstorm. The prisoners are not allowed to hide. On the contrary, they are beaten and pushed to march on.
The next day another 5 prisoners are shot after attempting to flee. The SS-men were ruthless.
Later 16 more men are shot. At a roll call, 30 men are missing. The Germans suspect that they hide in a haystack, and set fire to the haystack. When the prisoners appear with their hands behind their necks, they are mercilessly shot.
In addition, many die of exhaustion on the way.

How did Geert Greydanus experience this trip? Did his faith and the remembrance  of his wife and children get him through? It is a group of walking skeletons marching and marching on. Some Jewish prisoners remember a prayer:

	A thousand will fall on your left side,
	Ten thousand on your right side
	But nothing will happen to you
	With your own eyes you will see how I punish the wicked ..

On the night of April 10, the prisoners drag themselves through the main gate of Buchenwald camp. A SS-guard shouts: "Noch ein Haufen Dreck" ("one more pile of dirt").
They enter Barrack 47 and fall fast asleep, filled with fear as they don't know what will happen with them.
Many prisoners from Buchenwald have already been taken to Dachau, another death march.
Of the 525 prisoners that started the march from Niederorschel to Buchenwald more than a 100 die.
It is thanks to Otto Hermann that the group halted regularly. He caused the delays so that the group from Niederorschel arrived last. By then there are hardly any prisoners left in Buchenwald.

Otto Hermann is later posthumously awarded the Yad Vashem award for his activities.


(Images from "Aus dem Umkreis der Kamine")

The Americans arrive at Buchenwald the next day.

The SS-men still behave like beasts. But that changes when an American patrol unexpectedly arrives, they flee like scared rabbits.
Some SS-men are chased and lynched by prisoners and the Americans do not prevent it.
(There are SS-men, who only a few years later are teachers again, or pastors of a small community, or managers in a company. It is incomprehensible that they can live on as if nothing had happened.)

Suddenly there is a serene tranquility in the camp; gone are the screams they were so used to.

One of the first American soldiers to arrive at this camp is Milton Silva. He is confronted with an indescribable stench. From the gate he sees the great misery: hundreds of corpses and survivors looking like skeletons. He does not dare to enter.


The week before Geert returns to Buchenwald, the Nazis deported 23,000 people further east. Himmler's mission was to make sure that no living witnesses remain in the camps. Those too weak to be deported are killed on the spot, and that happened a lot.

General Eisenhower, who happens to be in the neighbourhood with his staff, wants to personally inspect the camp. So nothing is cleaned before his visit. He is shocked, and orders that everything be captured in photos. There will come a time, he predicts, when all this will be denied. He develops an intense hate towards the Germans who participated in this.

He then has the entire city of Weimar rounded up to show the camp to the citizens. In addition, he forces the citizens to clean up the corpses.

Geert Greydanus and Johannes Schepen both survived. They must have thought they were in hell. But they gradually gain strength again after carefully taking more food, and the Red Cross makes medical care available.
After a few weeks, Geert has recovered so much that he can return to the Netherlands. He arrives in Eindhoven on 9 May 1945. From Eindhoven he sends a message via the Red Cross to Johanna that he is alive but that he doesn't know anything else.

The Dutch authorities wanted to know what had happened to the many Dutch people who had been abroad.


He arrives in Rotterdam on 12 May.
Joy all around. With his history as a person in hiding, but much more his experiences in Germany, it is a wonder that Geert survived the war.

(Image courtesy Jenny Krabbe)


Back in Rotterdam

Johanna must have been shocked at the sight of Geert. He looks unrecognizable, missing a few teeth, clothes like rags and hollow eyes.
In the course of May he reports to the police station and is reappointed to his old position: major.
But a big disappointment awaits him. Many of his old colleagues have collaborated strongly with the Germans. There were even colleagues who were very active in tracing people in hiding and Jews. There is little interest in what Geert has experienced. He feels unhappy with this attitude.
His requests for extra food, concentration camp victims are entitled to this, are left unanswered. Assuming that the request has been lost, he writes again, but again no answer. Then he writes a letter “on legs”.
Getting back his personal belongings he had to hand over in Vught was also very difficult. Many victims experience the same problems. Questions were raised about this in the House of Representatives.



Letter written by Geert to get extra food stamps.
(Owned by NIOD)

And how did Hendrik Satter fare?
He was taken to Siegburg concentration camp. There he develops typhoid fever, usually a lethal disease in those circumstances. But miraculously Hendrik survives. He also returns after the war, but physically is a human wreck.
When he arrives home, he finds his farm burned down. It was in the field of fire between the Germans and the Canadians.
Hendrik thinks he was properly insured, but the insurance pays nothing: damages due to acts of war are never insured.

Hendrik has thus nothing left. His money was spent on the people in hiding, his farm is destroyed. And he himself is a wreck. It was an awful situation and he had strong feelings of resentment.


Geert is also unhappy and he is not alone. There are many Dutch people who had imagined life differently after the Second World War.
Many decided to turn their backs to the Netherlands and start a new life in another country. They emigrate, to America, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa or Canada.
Geert and Johanna decide to go to Canada. Together with two of Geert's brothers and their families, they prepare to start a new life on the other side of the Ocean. Geert decides to sign up as a farmer, because there is no work for police officers in Canada.
Jan and Jacob were now 19 and 18 years old. Geert and Jan immerse themselves in farm life.

Finally Geert and Johanna get permission to emigrate to Canada with the family. But Jaap, the eldest son, is conscripted and has to go to the Dutch East Indies. Whatever Geert tries, he cannot get Jaap off.

Then a new drama occurs in the lives of Geert and Johanna.
Jaap has been in the Dutch East Indies for barely 10 days when he gets wounded in a confusing fight between Dutch soldiers in Pasoeroean. Jaap died on 21 August 1947 due to blood loss.
He is buried in the military cemetery in Surabaya.


Jaap's grave in the cemetery of honor in Surabaya.

Fourteen days later on September 8, 1947, Geert and Johanna and their children get on the boat to Canada, without Jaap.



Canada

On September 21, 1947, Geert and his family arrive in Canada.
And it turned out not to be a land of milk and honey, as they might have hoped.

Wietske explains:

The farmer, Dalton Snyder, where they came in Baden (Ont.) had asked for a family with large children. Geert had to work long days, without even a day off now and then.
The oldest daughter Wietske (who provided this information) also had to work long hours for only $ 2.00 / day. Jan was soon fired. The family was not very happy and felt that they were being exploited.

Geert complains to the emigration officer.

They get a new place with a chicken farmer, Mr. Berkenshaw in Ridgetown.
They arrive at the end of March 1948.
Wietske remembers the day of May 8, 1948, the day before the 25th anniversary of Geert and Johanna.
There was a violent storm. The roof of the house, which looked bad and was poorly maintained anyway, began to leak, causing rainwater to pour into the house. Finally, part of the roof collapsed.
Geert went to the farmer for help, but the farmer refused to come.
Then Geert and Johanna take control and started looking for a better place to work themselves. They end up at a tomato grower, also in Ridgetown. With a new but very small house, with only 2 small bedrooms. Later they rent a bigger house a few kilometers north of Dresden. This is the first house with some luxuries like running water and even a bathroom.

On November 27, 1948, Wietske marries Dirk Walinga and moves in with the parents, so that they can jointly pay the rent of $ 35.00 / month.
But the following spring the intense rain saturates the ground and living in this house was no longer possible. They move again.
Wietske and Dirk leave for Dresden and Geert, Johanna and the other children went to live outside the city in an old house, again without any comfort.

It was now 1950. In that year Geert and Johanna moved to a house outside Chatham, while Wietske and Dirk moved to Wallaceburg.
Cor was now 14 years old and had been to 6 different schools in a short time.
Then Geert and Johanna are able buy their own company in Clinton in 1955, while Wietske and Dirk buy a farm in Londesboro in 1954. This family now has 3 children.
Wietske honestly admits that working on the farms was not a success. Partly due to lack of knowledge and on the other hand due to lack of money.
It had been a hard life until then, in several ways.

Geert also notes that emigrating to Canada has been a mistake. They should have stayed in Holland.


As his pension is paid in a lump-sum, Geert and Johanna decide to visit the Netherlands. Their trip overall takes three months.
They also visit the Satter family. Willemien Satter remembers that well. Geert looked like a real Canadian: he wore a jacket with a red lining!

The following story shows that Geert was a social person.
Like so many people in the fifties, Jippe and Jacoba Hoekstra (my parents) also had plans to emigrate to Canada with their 3 children.
Jacoba was a daughter of Jurjen Baron and sister of Wietske Baron, and thus a first cousin of Johanna.
The Canadian government required future emigrants to provide a bond in Canada. So Jacoba (Japke) wrote a letter to Geert and Johanna. The answer was very positive: Geert was perfectly willing to stand guarantee for the Hoekstra family from Holland! And this although he and Johanna had a very bad time themselves. They must have been very special people indeed.

The attached photo remains of the correspondence back and forth.
(The emigration of Jippe and Jacoba in the end never happened by the way.)

(Photo courtesy Jurjen Hoekstra)

This is the end of my story about Geert Greydanus.

Unfortunately, my original assumption that he was the one who brought Leni the Beer to Suawoude could not be confirmed of proved.

Geert did not grow very old, he died at 66 years of age.

Attached an image of the gravestone with the names of Geert Greydanus and Johanna Pebesma. They are buried in Clinton (Canada).
