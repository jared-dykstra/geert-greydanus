The betrayal: Willy Markus

The raid on the Satter farm on 20 April 1944 was one of many that day across the Achterhoek, all set off by Willy Markus, an informer for the German security police (the SD) who had spent three months inside the local resistance. The LO-LKP's own report on Varsseveld puts it plainly: "Willie Marcus ... The entire group of 'Black Kees' trusted him. On April 20, 1944, he struck. On that date, 3 persons were taken away here, namely Satter, Greydanus, and Schaap" (NIOD 251a inv. 20, scan 138, English rendering of the Dutch OCR). The Germans had a name for the Satter house: "het Broeinest", the hotbed (Oorlogsgravenstichting, Hendrik Satter).

Who Willy Markus was

Willem (Willy) Markus was born in Ambt Doetinchem on 24 March 1902. When the war began he ran the café his late father had built up at Hommelstraat 22 in Arnhem. It became a meeting place for black marketeers and German soldiers, and Markus bought drink for the German army and resold it at a profit. In 1943 he was jailed for receiving stolen goods, and came out as a V-Mann, a paid informer, for the SD. He learned the work from another Arnhem man, Johnny de Droog. (Wikipedia NL; Oorlogsbronnen)

How he worked, January – April 1944

  • Arnhem jail. His first job was to befriend a warder who smuggled notes out of the Arnhem prison for resistance prisoners. Markus and De Droog read the notes before they were delivered, and in January 1944 fourteen people were arrested in Velp and Rheden. (Wikipedia NL)

  • "Willy van Erp". In January 1944, under that name, he made contact with Gerrit van den Boogerd ("Oom Gert"), who led a military resistance group of more than 500 men. He passed himself off as a secret agent of the Dutch government in London. He won trust by bringing weapons, among them a carbine he put in the hands of Cornelis Ruizendaal ("Zwarte Kees"), leader of the Aalten knokploeg (KP), the armed squad. Because the Germans controlled some of London's radio links, he could even have agreed phrases broadcast on Radio Oranje. (Het Grote Gebod, pp. 482–484; Wikipedia NL)

  • Kees Poulie of Varsseveld. Poulie was a police sergeant at the Varsseveld post who worked with the LO and spread Trouw. On 4 January 1944 he helped Lt. Ford W. Babcock, an American airman who had come down near Sinderen, escape from the Germans, and went into hiding himself. He brought Babcock to the Satter farm that day; because the SD was searching the neighbourhood, Babcock was moved on to the Hofs farm next door (see the airman-helping network). Markus offered Poulie and his fiancée, Anna Droog, a way to England through a Belgian escape line. He met them in Zutphen on 8 March and had them arrested at Oosterbeek station the next morning. They were held in Kleve, then in the Vught bunker. A German police court in Vught sentenced Poulie to death on 2 June, and he was shot in the dunes at Overveen on 6 June 1944. (Westland, Marechaussee Contact, Oct 2020) What the SD got from his interrogation went onto its list of names (Wikipedia NL). Interpretation, not a source: Poulie knew the Satter and Hofs farms from the Babcock affair, which may be how the SD came to them.

20 April 1944

The SD struck on 20 April, a day before Zwarte Kees planned to attack the home-guard post in Ruurlo where Markus lodged. In the morning Markus picked up Van den Boogerd and his deputy Dick Vermeij, who were arrested first. He then drove a borrowed lorry to Beltrum to collect four KP men for a "weapons delivery". They grew suspicious when they saw blood on the floor of the lorry and got out at Doesburg. Two of them were caught at Ellecom; Zwarte Kees was killed in a gunfight in Doesburg. The chemist who had sheltered them, Philippus Gastelaars, was arrested at his shop in Doesburg and shot the next day, 21 April, at the Avegoor SS school in Ellecom (Oorlogsgravenstichting). In the afternoon the raids followed in Aalten, Eibergen, Lichtenvoorde, Lintvelde, Neede and Varsseveld. (Wikipedia NL; Het Grote Gebod, p. 484)

The next day 27 prisoners were sent from Arnhem to Vught. Four of them jumped from the lorry on the way, as the timeline's 20–21 April entry also records. Of the rest, about half died before a firing squad or in the camps; 22 men, ten of them from the Achterhoek, were shot at Overveen on 6 June 1944. A second wave of arrests followed on 30 April and 1 May. In all, more than 70 people fell victim to Markus's betrayal, and at least 28 of them did not survive. (Wikipedia NL; Westland 2020) A US Army helper file from Lichtenvoorde names him too: "By treachery of Willy Markens several workers have been shot" (Leemreize helper file).

After the war

Once the Achterhoek knew who had betrayed it, Markus was of no more use there. After a failed attempt in Roermond to trap a resistance leader who in turn hoped to kidnap him, he asked to be sent to work in Germany. After the war he feigned insanity and spent a long time in psychiatric institutions. On 5 July 1949 the Bijzonder Gerechtshof (special court) in Arnhem sentenced him to life imprisonment. The sentence was later cut to 18 years: in October 1950 according to Westland, in December 1952 according to Wikipedia. Westland adds that a further year was taken off in 1954. He was released in 1956 and died in Enschede on 24 March 1978. (Oorlogsbronnen; Wikipedia NL; Westland 2020)

What this adds for Geert

  • Markus's trial files are identified. The CABR (the archive of post-war collaboration cases at the Nationaal Archief) holds two files on Willem Markus of Arnhem: inventory number 66972 (file 1851), on paper and readable only in The Hague, and inventory number 188 (file 350/49), which is scanned and readable in the digital CABR at the Nationaal Archief and other reading rooms. The bodies involved were the special court and prosecutor in Arnhem, the Bijzondere Raad van Cassatie (the appeal court) and the political investigation service in Doetinchem. (Oorlog voor de Rechter) Such files often list the people a traitor gave up, and may show why the SD came to the Satter farm.

  • A possible second informer. The same LO-LKP Varsseveld report says: "There must have been another traitor hiding in Varsseveld, but this never came out. It was a certain 'Cor'. Under J. de Drooge, three worked in the Achterhoek, in Varsseveld Cor." It goes on to link a "Heezen", also called Cor Arends, to the arrest of the Jolink sisters, who hid Jews. (NIOD 251a inv. 20, scan 138, English rendering) Not identified; worth a search in the CABR index.

  • Geert's 1943 host was a different Wiggers. Het Grote Gebod lists "Hendrik Wiggers (Oom Henk)" among Markus's victims: Hendrik Jan Wiggers of Vellegendijk 17, Aalten, who hid a Jewish family and died at Mittelbau-Dora in February 1945 (Synagoge Aalten; Oorlogsbronnen). Geert's second hiding place, "bij H. Wiggers, hoeve Plak-an te Winterswijk", was another family's farm: Plakan, near the Bumerweg on the edge of Winterswijk, where the Jasmijnlaan now runs. Hans Wiggers (about 1911–2005) farmed there and sheltered Jews from Winterswijk and others until the end of the war; Yad Vashem honoured him on 10 October 1979. He came through the war unharmed. (OudWinterswijk; Achterhoek Nieuws, 4 May 2020)
