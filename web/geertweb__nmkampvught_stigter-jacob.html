Stigter, Jacob Born in: Honselersdijk on July 23, 1924 Died in: Camp Vught on August 9, 1944, 20 years

In 2002, a brother-in-law of Jaap sent us the following memories:

Writing something about someone's life after 60 years is not easy, even if you knew that person well. We will try.

Jaap was born on July 23, 1924, in Honselersdijk, in the municipality of Naaldwijk in the Westland. When he was born, he had a broken arm; that is what my wife—Jaap's sister—told me. After my wife and I got engaged on August 25, 1931, I met Jaap; he was 7 years old at the time. He was a cheerful, friendly boy, lighthearted, often carefree, and a daredevil.

When he was about four years old, he walked into a manure pit. His father saw his hand sticking out and was able to pull him out. When he was about 10 years old, he climbed onto the roof where the electrical wires ran. He lost his balance, grabbed onto the wires, and lost consciousness. He slid off the roof, and my brother-in-law Henk just managed to catch him. He soon regained consciousness and was left with only a sore hand.

When he was about 12 years old, he developed a very sore shinbone; it turned out to be a type of tuberculosis. To treat the leg, Jaap had to sit in a chair for two weeks. That was a difficult time for him. After his school years, he worked at his father's horticultural business, but that was not what he wanted to continue doing.
Jaap was very musical. In November 1938, a marching band was founded in the village of Honselersdijk, which he soon joined. Jaap played the trombone, and the band regularly marched through the village.

In May 1940, the Germans invaded the Netherlands; Jaap was 15 years old at the time. In 1943, the Germans issued a decree requiring all men aged 18 to 40 to report for forced labor in Germany. Jaap said: “Never!” and looked for a place to go into hiding. At that time, his father and mother, who were then still living on the horticultural farm and in the Bospolder in Honselersdijk, had a Jewish couple staying with them. If Jaap hadn't reported himself, a house search would not have been unthinkable. So, on June 23, 1943, I took Jaap to Zutphen by train. He had found a place with a farmer in Neede. How, I don't know. In those days, you just didn't ask or say too much. That was the last time I saw him; he never returned to the Westland. He did go to Amsterdam occasionally, where he would stay with Henk and Pie, his eldest sister.

Farming wasn't his thing. He apparently did do some studying, but I don't know much about that.
In Neede, Jaap joined the underground resistance. I know nothing about that; we had our own family and our hands full with ourselves. What I am about to mention is what I heard from that time. It seems that a group, including Jaap, raided the home of someone who was collaborating with the Germans and supposedly possessed weapons. However, no weapon was found. When the group left, the man fired after them, wounding Jaap. A doctor treated him and took him away in his car. On the way, both were taken prisoner. They were brought to the concentration camp in Vught.

In the summer of 1944, Father Stigter and I traveled to Arnhem one afternoon to gather some further information about Jaap. The only thing that became clear to us was that Jaap was imprisoned in Vught. During the winter of 1944–1945, we heard nothing from Jaap.
When the Germans surrendered on May 5, 1945, the newspapers could appear again, and I placed an advertisement in *Trouw* requesting information regarding Jaap. Then there was a boy from Groningen who contacted our minister, Rev. P. van Hoven, and informed him that Jaap had been executed. The minister came to tell us. It was a heavy blow, especially for his parents and his girlfriend.

When I visited the town hall in Naaldwijk one day, it turned out that they had indeed received news of what had happened there in the autumn of 1944. During the summer of 1945, we received a letter from Mr. Greidanus from Rotterdam. He was with the police there and had been taken prisoner because he refused to arrest Jews. He had met Jaap in the prison in Vught. Greidanus was later transported to Germany but survived the war.
In the summer of 1945, we spoke with Greidanus on a Saturday afternoon, and he told us something about his time in the prison in Vught. He said that Jaap had been a great support to him and other prisoners. In the evening, they seemed to be able to talk quietly to each other in the prison; I do not know how. Mr. Greidanus said: “When we were too sad, Jaap prayed for them all and for Queen and country.” Mr. Greidanus and his wife also visited Jaap’s parents.
I went with Father Stigter to a gathering in Vught at the place where the boys were executed. Queen Wilhelmina was present. Later, a memorial was unveiled, and we went there as well.

That Jaap died in the faith has been a great comfort to his parents, his girlfriend, and to all of us.
RM Eising / December 9, 2002

KP-Neede
Jaap Stigter lives in Neede during the war and is a gardener by profession. During the war, Jaap is a member of the KP-Neede. When Jaap carries out a raid with other resistance fighters, probably in collaboration with someone who has German contacts, they are shot at. The goal of the raid is to seize weapons, but none are found. During the ensuing exchange of gunfire, Jaap is wounded. Jaap is treated by a doctor; later, Jaap and the doctor are arrested together in the car. At that time, a resistance group had formed in the Achterhoek around reserve officer Gerrit van den Boogerd. In January 1944, Gerrit came into contact with Willy Marcus, who, under the name Willy van Erp, had infiltrated the Achterhoek resistance on behalf of the SD. Willy Marcus gained the resistance's trust by showing them weapons. When the Germans are informed of a plan to liquidate a Landwacht member from Ruurloo, they decide to intervene earlier. The Germans probably invaded the Achterhoek on April 20, 1944. Jaap may have been arrested on the same day; according to other sources, Jaap was not arrested until May 24, 1944. It is certain that Jaap was executed by firing squad in Vught on August 9, 1944.
